## What is SEO Copywriting?

SEO copywriting involves creating a page that’s built to persuade your audience to take action, but also gets ranked in Google.

And no, striking that balance is not easy.

As [Kaleena Stroud](https://www.linkedin.com/in/kaleenastroud/ "LinkedIn – Kaleena Stroud"), Content Marketing Manager at Triple Whale, said, “SEO copywriting is all about assembling things like Tetris.”

Mainly, that’s because it’s the only type of marketing writing that has to combine two main goals.

Marketing writing can be separated into three core buckets:

- **Content writing:** Includes blogs, guides, or newsletters with the goal to inform
- **Copywriting:** Any page with the goal to persuade someone to do something (like buy a product or book a meeting)
- **SEO copywriting:** Commercial pages at the intersection of persuasion and discoverability — these pages are built to convert, but also to be found in search
![Three buckets of marketing writing](https://api.backlinko.com/app/uploads/2026/08/three-buckets-of-marketing-writing.webp)

Getting your commercial pages ranked in Google or cited by AI takes more than dropping in a few keywords.

You need a page that serves the buyer first — because that’s what both Google and AI want to recommend to users.

Let’s go through the four steps:

1. Understand your buyer’s situation
2. Build your messaging strategy document
3. Write copy that converts
4. Build pages that get visibility in search and AI

## Step 1: Understand Your Buyer’s Situation

Before you write a single word, you need to know three things:

- Who exactly is landing on your page (and what are they feeling at that moment)?
- What are they searching for?
- What’s the rest of the web already telling them?

This is the research layer that everything else builds from.

### Do Audience Research

You don’t have to invent a new message. You just assemble it from what your buyers are already saying.

That’s the beauty of voice-of-customer (VoC) research.

### Find Keywords That Match Transactional Intent

Keyword research for SEO copywriting means confirming your buyer is ready to act (and finding the exact terms that signal that readiness).

That means [search intent](https://backlinko.com/hub/seo/search-intent "Search Intent and SEO: How to Optimize for User Goals") matters even more here.

![Mapping Keywords to Search Intent](https://api.backlinko.com/app/uploads/2025/04/mapping-keywords-to-search-intent.png)

Mapping Keywords to Search Intent

Product and landing pages normally serve transactional or navigational intent.

The searcher already knows the category or type of product they’re looking for. Now they’re looking for a place to buy.

Here’s how to find keywords with the right search intent.

First, **start with broad keywords, then narrow down**. Begin with your category keyword and drill down to the specific, product-level terms buyers use when they’re ready to purchase.

You can use a tool like [Semrush’s Keyword Magic Tool](https://www.semrush.com/analytics/keywordmagic/start?utm_campaign=seo-copywriting&utm_source=backlinko.com&utm_medium=referral "Free Keyword Tool: Find the Right Keywords for SEO & AI Search") to get ideas. First, add your category keyword. Then, filter the results to see keywords with Transactional or Navigational intent.

![](https://api.backlinko.com/app/uploads/2026/08/keyword-magic-tool-intent-filter.webp)

As you pick your keywords, **read the SERP to confirm the intent**.

What ranks tells you how Google reads the query.

For an HVAC business, “tankless water heater installation” looks transactional (especially since it’s a service they offer).

But the SERP is full of videos and how-to content, not service pages.

![](https://api.backlinko.com/app/uploads/2026/08/google-serp-tankless-water-heater-installation-videos.webp)

That’s a match for transactional search intent — and the keyword to target.

As you do this, **pull data from your audience research**. The pain points and use cases you gathered earlier point straight to keywords — the exact problems and situations buyers search for.

Remember: how you describe your product or service may not necessarily be how your audience describes it.

### Study What’s Already Being Said

When you understand the gaps in what’s already online, you can fill them with insight only you have.

This step is about building differentiation through competitor research.

Start by **reading the pages that rank** for your target keyword. Look at their messaging, claims, the angle they lead with, and who they’re writing for.

For example, these category pages both rank for terms like “moisturizers for dry skin.”

![](https://api.backlinko.com/app/uploads/2026/08/laroche-posay-and-cerave-same-language.webp)

And they basically repeat the same words over and over again.

That repetition is your map. The white space — what’s not being said — is where your product, expertise, or point of view can add something genuinely new.

Next, **check what AI is saying**.

Ask ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Google’s AI Mode about your product category and the problems it solves.

[Chris Shirlow](https://www.linkedin.com/in/chris-shirlow-64ab49275/ "LinkedIn – Chris Shirlow"), founder of FourHorse Digital, has built this into his process.

Those vague spots are exactly where your buyer needs a real answer, and where your firsthand expertise becomes the thing worth writing.

Keep all of this next to your audience research. It’s the raw material that will help you build your messaging strategy and write powerful copy.

## Step 2: Build Your Messaging Strategy Document

Research only pays off if it lands somewhere usable. The messaging doc turns scattered findings into a single source of truth that your whole team can use.

Here’s exactly how to build it.

### Set a Main Priority for This Page

With SEO copywriting you’re attacking two goals at once: rank in Google, and persuade a reader to convert.

That can create tension, which Yongi Barnard has felt firsthand.

What happens when both ranking and persuasion try to be equally dominant? In Yongi’s experience, the resulting page is lackluster.

So, start by pinning down three things:

- The page type
- Where this page sits in the customer journey
- How people find it

For example, a page buyers reach through branded search — like this Brooklinen product page — can lean more on creative, persuasive copy.

Synthesize Your Data

Right now, you’ve got a pile of raw material — Reddit threads, reviews, sales call notes, interview transcripts.

But before any of that can shape your copy, you need to find the patterns.

What pain points keep coming up? What phrases do buyers keep using to describe the issues they’re facing?

Most VoC quotes fall into a handful of buckets:

- **Objections and hesitations:** What makes them pause before buying

### Create a Clear Messaging Strategy Document

It’s time to turn everything you’ve gathered into one working document.

As Yongi Barnard puts it:

She structures her messaging strategy documents around four main parts:

First, **collect the must-knows**. These are the basics that frame everything else: the page type, its main goal, and where it sits in the customer journey.

Second, **add your core argument**. In one line, why this brand is the right fit for this specific audience. It’s basically the thesis of your landing page.

Third, **define the product details**. This includes things like:

- Positioning
- Value proposition
- Differentiators
- Main features
- Who it’s best for (and who it’s not for)
- How it works

Lastly, **add your audience information**. This is where your research pays off. Write down the main job to be done, motivations and pain points, and the most common objections.

![](https://api.backlinko.com/app/uploads/2026/08/seo-copywriting-messaging-toolkit-messaging.webp)

With everything in one place, this document serves more than a single page. It can give your whole team one consistent story to tell (exactly what AI citations thrive on).

## Step 3: Write Copy That Converts

It’s time for pure copywriting craft.

Every tactic here traces back to the research and messaging doc you put together in the previous steps. That way, you avoid the dreaded blank page — you can just put together copy from what you already know about your buyer and your product.

### Write From the Situation, Not the Feature List

A user searching with transactional intent wants to buy something.

Your job is to answer the questions that stand between them and a purchase:

- Does this fit my situation?
- Will it work for my use case?
- What happens if it’s wrong for me?

For example: “double-walled vacuum insulation” is a nice feature.

But what the buyer wants is “ice-cold water for 24 hours, even in a hot car.”

The second version puts the buyer in a moment they recognize (and often mirrors real search queries).

To start, look at the pain points and use cases you mapped above. What’s the tipping point where someone decides it’s time to search for a solution?

Here’s a great example from bookkeeping service business, Bench:

![](https://api.backlinko.com/app/uploads/2026/08/bench-bookkeeping.webp)

This page speaks directly to the audience’s situation and main pain points: they’re behind on bookkeeping and taxes, and they’re too overwhelmed to do it on their own. That’s a prime example of using your buyers’ exact language.

The sharpest situation-first copy even names who the product isn’t for. It feels counterintuitive, but calling out the wrong-fit buyer builds trust with the right one. And it can filter your traffic down to the people who are more likely to convert.

Of course, while writing your page’s copy, you need to keep your balance between functionality and value.

As content strategist [Emily Gertenbach](https://www.linkedin.com/in/egertenbach/ "LinkedIn – Emily Gertenbach") puts it:

In the real world, that means answering the questions your audience needs to know before purchasing, but without adding unnecessary fluff.

That’s exactly what Caraway does with their product pages. They answer common questions searchers are asking, like:

- Will this pan work on an induction cooktop?
- Can I use this pan in the oven?
- What’s the pan made of?
- How do I clean this pan?

Lean on your keyword research: What questions are people actually asking? That tells you what information should go on your commercial page.

Being comprehensive is table stakes now. And in most cases, that won’t be enough to rank in Google or get cited by AI.

You already studied what’s being said in Step 1. Now, it’s time to include what the rest of the web is leaving out. Take a stance, give advice based on your expertise, or include details that your competitors are glossing over.

### Work in Your Keywords (Without Ruining the Copy)

Your title tag, meta description, and H1 are your buyer’s first point of contact. So they do two jobs at once: signal the keyword and speak to the buyer’s pains.

Here’s how to fit keywords without flattening your copy.

Write the persuasive copy first. Then, once it’s down, look for natural places to add your keywords, like:

- Crossheads (H2s and H3s)
- FAQs
- Product or service descriptions
- Objection-handling sections
