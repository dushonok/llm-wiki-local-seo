Find Your Ideal Client Instead of Waiting for Upwork to Kick In

⚠️ WARNING: LONG READ FOR THE HUNGRY & FOCUSED

This idea was partly inspired by

[@Nadya Ershova](https://www.skool.com/@88816c0a359c4a5eb73166ce87f5ba39?g=rank-expand-academy)

and the video she shared today about finding and approaching potential clients through Upwork. And of course, credit to

[@Jesse Cunningham](https://www.skool.com/@91f6fa2c717d4a7aba092e2ab3e3b635?g=rank-expand-academy)

and

[@Shawn Buckles](https://www.skool.com/@647f49a878514a32a60ef5cc363526b4?g=rank-expand-academy)

for creating the Upwork profile model we're following here. I also believe

[@Blair Witkowski](https://www.skool.com/@ab346ce88d4e48cb932c684753540b53?g=rank-expand-academy)

has talked about doing something similar in his own way.

Before I get into it, I used Wispr Flow and some AI to help organize my thoughts because I wanted to get this out to you tonight. My goal is simple: help you create income while you're waiting for your sites to season and give you another way to find businesses you can genuinely help.

There are a LOT of business owners out there who need to grow. You're learning skills here that can make a real impact on those businesses in a relatively short amount of time. I do this for a living, and one of the most rewarding parts is watching something you build completely change the direction of someone's livelihood.

Blair said something I strongly agree with in his recent YouTube interview on Edward Sturm: if you're going to take somebody's money, you better be able to create value. If you're going to take the king's gold, you need to know what you're doing.

That's what this post is about.

I'm not suggesting you abandon Upwork, and I'm not saying you need to chase a $297/month offer like the one you'll see in the video below. Start smaller. Start bigger. Build what you're already learning to build. The point is to find people you can actually provide value to instead of only waiting for them to find you.

I think building a strong Upwork profile and going after opportunities there absolutely makes sense.

But it got me thinking:

While we're waiting for Upwork to kick in, why aren't we also going out and finding the exact businesses we'd actually WANT as clients?

Upwork is getting more competitive, and when a good job gets posted, you're often competing with a bunch of other people for the same opportunity.

With this strategy, you're finding the opportunity before they ever post a job looking for someone.

I was watching another video this evening that reinforced this idea, and I'll link it below.

Start with Google Maps

Pick a service based niche you understand or would actually like to work with.

Search for it on Google Maps.

But don't focus on the businesses already dominating the top three.

Keep going deeper.

Look at the businesses buried farther down in the Google Business Profile results.

Those could actually be some of your best prospects.

You may find businesses that:

• Have a significant review gap compared with the competitors outranking them

• Haven't received or responded to reviews consistently

• Have an outdated or poorly optimized website

• Don't have pages targeting their highest value services

• Have weak local SEO signals or missing schema

• Have a website and GBP that aren't properly aligned

• Aren't showing up for searches that could generate their highest value customers

And here's something else I'm betting you'll find pretty often as you get farther down:

A lot of these smaller businesses are probably using their actual mobile phone as the main number on their Google Business Profile.

Look at the website. Look at the business. You can usually get a pretty good sense when you're dealing with a smaller operation or an owner who is still personally running most of the business.

Instead of calling and potentially getting routed through a receptionist or office staff, you may already have a direct text line to the owner and decision maker sitting right there on Google.

Here's how I would test it

If you can find the owner's name on their website, GBP, About page, or somewhere else, use it:

"Hey, is this John, the owner of ABC Plumbing?"

If you can't find the owner's name, don't let that stop you:

"Hey, is this the owner of ABC Plumbing?"

That's it.

Don't sell anything yet.

If they respond yes, you just learned something pretty valuable:

The number works, it accepts texts, and you're probably talking directly to the decision maker.

Now here's where I'd borrow another strategy I've been using myself from Chris Voss.

Instead of asking a traditional yes based question like:

"Would you like me to send you a video?"

Try giving them the opportunity to say NO.

Something like:

"Great! I was looking at your business online and noticed a few things that may be keeping you from showing up higher for some valuable searches in your market. Would you object to me sending you a quick two minute video showing you a few things you could potentially improve to get seen by more customers?"

Now the comfortable answer is:

"No, I wouldn't object."

And you've received permission to send the video without making them feel like they just agreed to a sales call.

That's one of the things I like about Chris Voss's no oriented question strategy and something I've been incorporating into my own client communication.

People often feel safer saying no than being pushed into saying yes.

You're not asking them to buy anything.

You're not asking them to schedule an appointment.

You're basically asking:

"Would you object if I showed you what I'm seeing?"

And don't make the Loom until they give you permission

This was one of my favorite takeaways from the video I watched tonight.

And when they do give you permission, this isn't a 15 or 20 minute presentation.

Make it around two minutes.

That's the whole point.

If you reach out to ten businesses and two owners are open to seeing it, make two short Looms.

Pull up their GBP.

Pull up their website.

Show them where they're ranking.

Show them who's above them.

Show them the review gap.

Show them the missing service pages.

Show them where their GBP and website aren't supporting each other.

Show them one or two opportunities they can immediately understand.

Then you're done.

And most importantly:

Don't spend half the video talking about yourself.

They don't care about us yet.

Make those two minutes about their business, their problems, and their opportunity.

No fancy intro.

No music.

No giant presentation.

Just:

"Hey John, thanks for letting me send this over. Here's what I noticed..."

And show them.

The offer doesn't have to be expensive either

The creator in the video talks about a $297 a month offer, but that's his business model.

Maybe you find a smaller business that can't afford a huge website project.

Could you build them a simple site or additional targeted sites for $50 or $100 a month?

Could you eventually build multiple properties for them targeting different services or areas?

Could you start small, prove that you can help them, and gradually build out more assets so they can strengthen their presence across their territory and start moving higher in the results?

That's where I think this gets interesting.

You're not necessarily trying to land some massive contract from the first text.

You're trying to get the conversation started.

And the service doesn't have to be websites either.

The same client acquisition strategy could work for:

SEO, website design, GBP optimization, reputation management, content, automation, AI, lead generation, Google Ads, CRM setup, or whatever service you specialize in.

What if you did just 2 or 3 of these every day?

Pick a niche you actually want to work with.

Find businesses buried in Maps.

Look at their website and GBP.

Find the ones where you can clearly see an opportunity.

See if that published number looks like it could be going directly to the owner.

Send one simple text:

"Hey, is this John, the owner of ABC Plumbing?"

Or:

"Hey, is this the owner of ABC Plumbing?"

Once you know you're talking to the right person, use the no oriented question:

"Would you object to me sending you a quick two minute video showing you a few things I noticed that could potentially help you get seen by more customers?"

Only make the Loom when they're open to receiving it.

Give them something genuinely useful.

Then start the conversation.

[@Nadya Ershova](https://www.skool.com/@88816c0a359c4a5eb73166ce87f5ba39?g=rank-expand-academy)

Definitely build your Upwork profile. Definitely go after opportunities there.

But while you're waiting for Upwork to kick in, you could also spend a little time every day finding the exact type of client you want to work with and starting the conversation yourself.

Upwork can be one fishing pole in the water. It doesn't have to be the only one.
